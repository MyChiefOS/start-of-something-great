# Second Look: what the public frameworks ask for (research, Oct 30, 2026)

Research for Aaron's decision of Oct 30 (harness/FACTS.md line 193): Second Look decides within limits, a very small in-house model, informed by public frameworks. This is research, not legal advice. Labels: **[V]** verified (I read the source named), **[I]** inferred (and from what), **[U]** unknown.

---

## 0. Bad news and conflicts first

1. **The site's own public promises are the binding constraint, not the AI laws.** [V] privacy.html line 73 says a high-risk post "is held … until a person reviews it" and "Decisions that significantly affect you are made or reviewed by a person". terms.html line 48 says the same, and team.html line 76 says of JEVs "no AI decides". Once Second Look clears a held post, the first sentence is false. [I] In the US, a public statement about how a service works that stops being true is the classic FTC Act section 5 "deception" exposure. Before Second Look acts on anything (shadow mode is fine, because it acts on nothing), /privacy, /terms and /team have to say plainly that a narrow AI model may release some held posts, which ones, and that a person decides appeals. FACTS.md already says "decide within limits" changes the "no AI decides" promise. This adds that two legal pages carry the same promise.
2. **Training on members' posts isn't disclosed, and the deletion promise gets harder.** [V] privacy.html says that when a post is deleted, "our checks erase what they kept about it", and nothing on the page mentions training a model. [I] If Second Look learns from held posts and Aaron's decisions, then (a) privacy.html must say so before any training data is collected, and (b) a deleted post's text can't simply be erased from trained weights. The cheapest design that keeps the promise: train only on features, or on text kept for a short, stated time; drop deleted posts from the training set; retrain on a schedule; and say all this on the page. Every framework below lists data governance (NIST MEASURE 2.x, ISO 42001's data controls, OECD 1.5 traceability, Singapore's data lineage).
3. **"Release only" doesn't make a mistake harmless.** [I] The limit protects the poster, who is never hidden or banned by the AI. It does not protect readers or the people a post is about. A false release is the harm that counts: a doxxing post (J08 contact details), a threat, a leaked key (J18), or an intimate image someone has asked to have taken down. Every framework's oversight test turns on the severity of the harm, not on which direction the action goes (Singapore's probability × severity matrix; NIST MAP 5.1). Shadow mode should therefore graduate on the **false-release rate**: how often Second Look would have released a post Aaron kept held or removed. Raw agreement isn't the measure. Some categories should never be eligible for Second Look at all (section 4).
4. **A standing-decision row needs Aaron's wording, but no re-decision.** [V] CHARTER.md section 2, Oct 16 row: "high risk is held for Aaron"; Oct 14 row: "Aaron moderates member posts". His Oct 30 decision (decide within limits) narrows both. It isn't a conflict to re-ask him about, but the charter rows should be amended by PR to record the change, or later sessions will read Second Look as violating them.
5. **ISO bars its content from AI use.** [V] ISO's copyright page: apart from ISO Open Data, "no ISO content may be used for any machine learning", which includes prompting AI tools. So Aaron (or a human he trusts) can buy and read ISO/IEC 42001 and 23894, but their text must never be pasted into Claude, into Second Look's training data or into the repository. What I give below on ISO is from general knowledge, in my own words, labelled [I].
6. **One source was wrong.** A law blog (promise.legal) said every Texas deployer must disclose AI use. [V] Baker Botts says the enacted TRAIGA limits that duty to government agencies and health care. I relied on Baker Botts.

## 1. Framework by framework

### 1.1 NIST AI RMF 1.0, plus the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1)
- **Current version:** [V] AI RMF 1.0 (Jan 26, 2023). NIST's page says it "is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan". No draft, number or date has been published. Companion: AI 600-1, Generative AI Profile (Jul 26, 2024). Newest item: a concept note for a critical-infrastructure profile (Apr 7, 2026). Source: [NIST AI RMF page](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework).
- **Reuse:** [V] "Works authored by NIST employees are not subject to Copyright protection within the United States"; foreign rights are reserved, with a royalty-free worldwide reprint right; attribution is requested ("Republished courtesy of the National Institute of Standards and Technology"). Third-party content inside NIST documents may be copyrighted. **Can quote and adapt in the MIT repo, with attribution.** Source: [NIST copyright statement](https://www.nist.gov/open/copyright-fair-use-and-licensing-statements-srd-data-software-and-technical-series-publications).
- **What applies to Second Look** [V, subcategory text from [NIST AIRC Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/) and [myctrl.tools](https://www.myctrl.tools/frameworks/nist-ai-rmf/family/measure)]:
  1. Human oversight is defined and documented: MAP 3.5, and GOVERN 3.2 (roles for "human-AI configurations").
  2. Valid and reliable before deployment, under deployment-like conditions, with limits on generalising noted: MEASURE 2.3, 2.5. Safety is evaluated regularly and the system "can fail safely": MEASURE 2.6.
  3. Explained and documented, i.e. a model card: MEASURE 2.9. Bias evaluated: MEASURE 2.11.
  4. Feedback and appeal for affected people: MEASURE 3.3. Incidents communicated, including to affected communities: MANAGE 4.3. Post-deployment monitoring: MANAGE 4.1.
  5. Mechanisms "to supersede, disengage, or deactivate" (the off switch), with incident thresholds that trigger it: MANAGE 2.4. Safe decommissioning: GOVERN 1.7.
  6. Risk tolerance documented: MAP 1.5. Likelihood and magnitude of each impact: MAP 5.1.
- **AI 600-1** [V, [NIST AI 600-1 PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)]: 12 GenAI risks, of which these matter here: Confabulation, Dangerous/Violent/Hateful Content, Data Privacy, Harmful Bias, Human-AI Configuration, Information Security (prompt injection), Obscene/Abusive Content. Four primary considerations: Governance, Content Provenance, Pre-deployment Testing, Incident Disclosure. Useful actions: MS-2.7-007 (red-team, including prompt injection: a held post may contain text aimed at the model); MS-2.5-005 (verify training-data provenance); MS-2.8-002 (document the instructions given to annotators); GV-1.7-001 (able to be deactivated); GV-4.3-002 (minimum criteria for incident reporting).

### 1.2 OMB guidance (US federal agencies only)
- **Current:** [V] **M-25-21** (Apr 2025) "rescinds and replaces" M-24-10. M-25-22 covers procurement (it replaced M-24-18 [I], from the same law-firm summaries; not opened). M-26-04, "Unbiased AI Principles" (Dec 2025), applies to agencies buying LLMs [V, title and date from the [whitehouse.gov PDF listing](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/M-26-04-Increasing-Public-Trust-in-Artificial-Intelligence-Through-Unbiased-AI-Principles-1.pdf); body not read]. Source: [M-25-21](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf).
- **Applies to us?** [V] No: it governs agencies' own use of AI.
- **Reuse:** [I] US government work, so public domain in the US (17 U.S.C. 105). Can quote and adapt.
- **Useful as a model** [V]: section 6 presumes that "the blocking, removal, hiding, or limitation of the reach of protected speech" is high-impact when an agency does it. So JEVs's holds sit in a category OMB treats as high-impact, and Second Look's releases undo a limitation. The minimum practices for high-impact AI: pre-deployment testing; an AI impact assessment with independent review and a signed acceptance of the risk; ongoing monitoring and periodic human review; operator training; human oversight, intervention and accountability; "timely human review and a chance to appeal"; public feedback; and stopping any use that falls short of these practices. Footnote 27: a system is high-impact only if its output is the "principal basis" for the decision.

### 1.3 EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended by the Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744)
- **Current:** [V] The omnibus was published Jul 24, 2026 and in force from Jul 27, 2026 ([Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/digital-ai-omnibus-delays-key-deadlines-introduces-new-rules/); [Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force)). Annex III high-risk duties move to **Dec 2, 2027**; Annex I to Aug 2, 2028. Article 50 transparency applies from **Aug 2, 2026**, with a grace period to Dec 2, 2026 for marking under 50(2) for systems already on the market. AI literacy (Art. 4) is softened to "support". A new Art. 5 ban on systems that generate non-consensual intimate content or CSAM applies from Dec 2, 2026. Art. 6(3) registration was kept, in a simplified form.
- **Reuse:** [I] EU legislation can be reused freely with acknowledgment (the Commission's reuse decision 2011/833/EU, which I recall but didn't open). Can quote and adapt.
- **What would apply if we were in scope** [V, [Art. 14](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14), [Art. 50](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50), [Annex III](https://ai-act-browser.ch.innoq.io/annex/III)]: Second Look is not in Annex III, so Art. 14's human-oversight rules (understand limits, guard against "automation bias", override or reverse, a "stop" button) are good practice for us, not law. Art. 50(1) applies to systems that "interact directly with natural persons", and Second Look doesn't. Art. 50(2) and (4) apply to systems that generate synthetic content or public-interest text, and Second Look's job is a release decision [I].

### 1.6 OECD AI Principles
- **Current:** [V] OECD/LEGAL/0449, adopted May 22, 2019 and amended May 3, 2024 ([OECD PDF](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/public/doc/648/648.en.pdf)).
- **Reuse:** [V] Free; "may be reproduced and distributed without further permission" if it is not altered and not sold. **Quote verbatim with attribution; don't adapt the text itself.** Our own requirements can paraphrase it.
- **What applies** [V]: 1.3, information that lets those adversely affected "challenge its output"; 1.4, systems can be "overridden, repaired, and/or decommissioned safely", with human intervention where needed; 1.5, traceability of "datasets, processes and decisions" and systematic, ongoing risk management.

### 1.7 Singapore: Model AI Governance Framework, 2nd ed. (2020), and the Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (Jan 22, 2026; updated May 20, 2026)
- **Current:** [V] 2nd edition (Jan 2020). Agentic framework launched at WEF Jan 22, 2026 ([Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/01/singapore-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai-launched)), with an "updated" version on May 20, 2026 ([IMDA factsheet](https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/factsheets/2026/updated-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai); what changed is [U]). There is also a GenAI framework (May 2024) [I].
- **Reuse:** [U] I didn't read the IMDA/PDPC terms. Cite and paraphrase until they're checked.
- **What applies** [V]: (1) The degree of human involvement is set by a probability × severity-of-harm matrix, weighed with reversibility and recourse. Human-out-of-the-loop is acceptable for "low severity and probability of harm"; high severity calls for human-in-the-loop ([Wong Partnership summary](https://wongpartnership.com/upload/medias/KnowledgeInsight/document/9013/LegisWatch-IntroductionofaModelArtificialIntelligenceGovernanceFramework.pdf); [AIGL summary](https://www.aigl.blog/model-artificial-intelligence-governance-framework-second-edition.md)). (2) Agentic: "bound risks upfront" by limiting tools, permissions and action scope; oversight must be able to "override, intercept or review"; test before deployment; "stage progressive roll-outs"; monitor in real time; tell users what the system can do and whom to contact. (3) Approval gates for high-stakes and irreversible actions; automation bias is named ("over-trust … especially when it has performed reliably in the past"); track override rates ("a low rate may signal rubber-stamping"); **no numeric thresholds** ([Modulos summary of §2.2](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/singapore-mgf-agentic/human-accountability)).

## 2. Combined checklist

"Mandatory" here means a duty that applies to Day One Great today. **No AI-specific framework is legally mandatory for us** [I]. NIST, ISO, IEEE, OECD and Singapore are voluntary. OMB binds agencies only. The EU AI Act and the DSA reach providers whose output is used in the Union, and we decline EU/EEA/UK/CH sign-ups (CHARTER Oct 16). Even in scope, Second Look would be minimal-risk, and an AI system released under a free and open-source licence is exempt unless it is high-risk, prohibited or covered by Art. 50 (Art. 2(12) [V]). Colorado's and California's laws exclude moderation or don't list it. Texas has intent-based bans only. What *is* mandatory comes from general law: keep our public statements true (FTC Act §5 [I]) and run TAKE IT DOWN removals [I, covered-platform status inferred].

## 3. High-risk classification and transparency duties

- **EU AI Act:** [V] Not prohibited (Art. 5) and not in Annex III (no content-moderation area). [I] So minimal-risk. Art. 50 doesn't apply, because Second Look neither converses with people nor publishes generated content. The only general duty is Art. 4 AI literacy, now softened. If the code and weights are released under MIT, Art. 2(12) may exempt it entirely [I].
- **US:** [I] No federal or state rule classifies post moderation as high-risk for a private site. Colorado SB 26-189 excludes content moderation [V]. California's ADMT list doesn't include it [V]. Texas has intent-based bans only [V]. OMB would treat agency speech-limiting as presumed high-impact [V], but it doesn't bind us. Section 230 protects good-faith moderation choices, whether to leave a post up or take it down [I].
- **If the site ever served the EU:** [I] The Digital Services Act, more than the AI Act, would change things. Art. 17 [V]: every restriction (JEVs's hold, Aaron's removal) needs a statement of reasons that includes "the use of automated means". A release isn't a restriction, so Second Look's own decisions mostly wouldn't trigger it. Art. 14 terms must describe algorithmic decision-making and human review [I]. Art. 20 (internal complaints, not decided solely by automated means) and the rest of Section 3 don't apply to micro and small enterprises (Art. 19) [V]. GDPR Art. 22 would cover solely automated decisions with significant effects; a hold may qualify, a release likely doesn't [I]. Article 50, AI literacy and the Dec 2026 nudifier ban would apply directly [V].

## 4. Learning from few decisions; when it may act without a human

**What the frameworks say** [V unless marked]:
- None of the frameworks I read gives a sample size or agreement threshold for graduating a system. Singapore's agentic framework says so explicitly; for the others, none appeared in the sections I read [U whether one exists elsewhere].
- The test that applies is **severity × probability, reversibility and recourse** (Singapore 2nd ed.). Out-of-the-loop is acceptable only for low severity and low probability. Releasing a post is reversible for the platform, but not for a person whose phone number was shown for an hour.
- **Staged autonomy:** "stage progressive roll-outs" and bound the action space (Singapore agentic); show validity under deployment-like conditions and note limits on generalising (NIST MEASURE 2.3, 2.5); stop any use that falls short (OMB); use automation only with "sufficiently high confidence" and publish accuracy (Santa Clara).
- **Human labels:** AI 600-1 MS-1.1-008 (structured human feedback) and MS-2.8-002 (document annotator instructions); the human labeller here is Aaron. Watch automation bias once Aaron sees Second Look's suggestions: if his labels start copying the model, the training data stops being independent (Singapore's rubber-stamping signal) [I].

**Concrete graduation rule** [I, my design from the above plus standard binomial statistics]:
1. **Eligible holds only.** Holds driven by context questions (J01 account age, J02 sign-in strength, J04 activity rate, J07 links and new domains, J13 region) are eligible. Holds involving J08 contact details, J09 copies, J10 track record, J14 credible reports, J18 keys, any takedown request or any appeal are never eligible. Aaron sets this list; it lives in code, not in the model.
2. **Shadow mode measures false release, per category.** Graduate a category when Second Look's 95% upper bound on false release is below Aaron's tolerance. With zero false releases in *n* eligible cases, the 95% upper bound is about 3/*n*: 100 cases bounds it under 3%, 300 cases under 1%. One false release in 300 bounds it at about 1.6%. At today's volume (the site went live Oct 29) that could take months [I]. That's a reason to keep the daily cap low and the category list short at first.
3. **Small model, few labels:** with few of Aaron's decisions, a model this small will overfit [I]. Use public human-labelled datasets for general toxicity and spam (each licence checked), use Aaron's decisions to calibrate the release threshold, and hold out a test set he labelled that the model never trains on.
4. **After graduation:** keep random spot-checks (for example 1 in 10 releases goes to Aaron afterwards), track his reversal rate, and trip the off switch automatically on a reversed harmful release. These alert him and never lock him out.
